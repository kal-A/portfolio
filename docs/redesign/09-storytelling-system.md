# Storytelling System

**Depends on:** [00 Portfolio Vision](./00-portfolio-vision.md), [07 Case Study System](./07-case-study-system.md), [08 Diagram System](./08-diagram-system.md)  
**Purpose:** A consistent voice and argument structure across the homepage and case studies

## Core narrative

The portfolio's recurring story is:

> I make complex, real-world product situations easier to understand and act on by combining research, systems thinking, product judgment, and execution.

Each project proves a different part of that statement. No project should attempt to prove everything.

## Portfolio proof map

Before editing pages, assign each featured project:

- one primary competency;
- one secondary competency;
- one memorable decision;
- one credible outcome;
- one limitation or learning.

Choose the featured set so the projects complement rather than repeat one another.

## Argument unit

Build sections using this sequence:

1. **Claim:** What should the reviewer understand?
2. **Evidence:** What observation, artifact, or result supports it?
3. **Interpretation:** Why did that evidence matter?
4. **Decision:** What changed because of it?
5. **Consequence:** What happened, remained uncertain, or was learned?

Not every unit needs five visible subheadings. The logic must still be present.

## Page-level story

### Homepage

The homepage answers:

1. What does Kamal do?
2. What kind of judgment does he bring?
3. Which projects best prove it?
4. Why should a reviewer keep reading or make contact?

Project summaries should be specific enough to distinguish the stories. Avoid repeating the same “designed a solution that improved user experience” formulation.

### About

The about page explains the through-line in Kamal's work, the environments in which he is effective, and what he is seeking. Personal interests may appear briefly when they explain perspective, but must not become a separate lifestyle portfolio.

### Case study

Each case study is an argument about judgment, not a chronological diary. Compress low-value activity and expand decisions, tradeoffs, collaboration, and outcomes.

## Voice

Write with calm specificity.

### Use

- active voice;
- concrete nouns and verbs;
- short section theses;
- honest first-person authorship;
- plain-language explanations of domain terms;
- qualified claims;
- reflection that changes future behavior.

### Avoid

- “passionate,” “innovative,” “user-centric,” “seamless,” and “leveraged” without evidence;
- hype, superlatives, and startup-marketing tone;
- self-deprecation or apology;
- academic density when plain language is available;
- method names as substitutes for insight;
- vague causal claims such as “resulted in” without supporting evidence.

## Heading rules

Headings should advance the story.

Prefer:

- “Scheduling failures began before users reached the calendar”
- “A smaller scope protected the critical handoff”
- “We traded flexibility for a faster first release”

Avoid:

- “Research”
- “The Problem”
- “Ideation”
- “Final Solution”

Short labels may still identify the chapter, but the main heading should carry the claim.

## Captions

A strong caption includes:

1. what the viewer is seeing;
2. what to notice;
3. why it matters to the decision.

Bad: “User flow.”  
Better: “The original flow required three ownership handoffs before confirmation; the highlighted path removed the highest-failure transition.”

## Metrics and evidence language

Label evidence accurately:

- **Measured:** observed after release with a stated method/timeframe.
- **Directional:** small sample or early signal.
- **Estimated:** model or assumption, not observed behavior.
- **Target:** intended future state.
- **Qualitative:** interview, usability, stakeholder, or field evidence.

Always state the denominator, baseline, timeframe, or sample when it changes interpretation.

## Collaboration story

Show collaboration through disagreement, alignment, constraints, and changed decisions—not a list of meetings. Name the perspective another person or discipline added and how it affected the result.

## HCI and graduate-review relevance

Without creating a separate admissions voice, preserve evidence of:

- research question and method fit;
- awareness of bias and limitations;
- interpretation beyond surface observations;
- connection between human behavior and system design;
- reflection and future questions.

Hiring reviewers receive the same value as proof of rigorous judgment.

## Editing process

For each page:

1. Write the one-sentence argument.
2. List the 3–5 proof points required.
3. Assign each proof point one primary artifact.
4. Build headings that communicate the argument when scanned.
5. Draft body copy only after the scan path works.
6. Remove repeated context, method theater, and unsupported claims.
7. Read captions and headings alone; confirm the story survives.
8. Test with a reviewer unfamiliar with the project.

## Five-second test

At every viewport, a new reviewer should understand:

- where they are;
- what this page or section is about;
- why the visible evidence matters;
- what action is available next.

If the answer requires reading a long paragraph, revise the heading, layout, or evidence.

## Anti-examples

- A hero that communicates mood but not professional value.
- A project cover that names a tool but not a problem.
- A research section that lists eight methods and no changed decision.
- A result section that celebrates launch but omits adoption or uncertainty.
- An about page that attempts to explain every interest.
- A caption that only restates the UI label visible in the image.

## Success criteria

- The homepage and each case study have one clear argument.
- Featured projects prove distinct competencies.
- Headings and captions form a coherent scan path.
- Individual contribution and team outcome remain distinguishable.
- Metrics and claims are qualified and defensible.
- Reflection demonstrates growth rather than generic hindsight.
