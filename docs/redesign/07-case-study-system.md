# Case Study System

**Depends on:** [00 Portfolio Vision](./00-portfolio-vision.md), [03 Layout System](./03-layout-system.md), [06 Component System](./06-component-system.md)  
**Purpose:** A repeatable story and layout that proves judgment, contribution, and outcome

## Goal

Let a busy reviewer understand the project in under five minutes while preserving enough depth for a hiring panel, design leader, or HCI faculty reviewer to evaluate the reasoning.

## Required answers

Every case study must make these answers explicit:

- What was the problem and why did it matter?
- Who experienced it?
- What was Kamal's role and scope?
- What constraints shaped the work?
- What evidence changed the team's understanding?
- What decisions did Kamal influence or own?
- What was made or changed?
- What happened afterward?
- What would he do differently now?

## Two reading modes

### Scan path

A reviewer reading only the title, abstract, metadata, headings, metrics, diagrams, captions, and final reflection must understand the complete argument.

### Deep path

Body copy, research detail, alternatives, and artifact evidence provide depth without changing the top-level story.

## Canonical structure

### 1. Project cover

- Project number and title.
- One-sentence thesis written as problem + intervention + result where defensible.
- Role/category, timeline, team, and status.
- One purposeful hero artifact or product view.
- Optional jump to summary/chapter index.

### 2. Executive summary

Use 3–5 short blocks:

- context;
- problem;
- my contribution;
- outcome;
- key constraint or caveat.

This is not a duplicate introduction. It is the five-minute reviewer's map.

### 3. Outcome metrics

Show 2–4 large plain numbers with captions beneath, not cards. Include units, timeframe, baseline, source, and qualifier. If there is no credible numeric outcome, use concise qualitative evidence rather than inventing a metric.

### 4. Context and stakes

Explain the user, workflow, organization, and why the problem deserved attention. Keep domain background to what the next decision requires.

### 5. Role, team, and constraints

State what Kamal owned, influenced, supported, and did not own. Include meaningful technical, organizational, time, access, and data constraints.

### 6. Discovery and reframing

Show the strongest evidence that changed the initial understanding. Research methods are secondary to what was learned and how confidence was established.

### 7. Decisions and tradeoffs

This is the core of the story. For each major decision:

1. signal or constraint;
2. interpretation;
3. alternatives considered;
4. decision and rationale;
5. consequence or risk.

### 8. Execution

Show how the decision became a workflow, prototype, feature, test, launch, or organizational change. Use before/after states and annotated product evidence.

### 9. Outcome and validation

Separate:

- shipped output;
- measured outcome;
- observed qualitative response;
- remaining uncertainty.

Do not imply causation when only correlation or directional evidence exists.

### 10. Reflection

State what changed in Kamal's thinking, what would be done differently, and what the project suggests about future work. Avoid generic “communication is important” lessons.

### 11. Next project

Recommend one relevant project that adds a different proof point.

## Narrative layout

- Use a strict two-column rhythm for major text–media pairs.
- Align the paragraph and its evidence at the same vertical position.
- Alternate sides when it improves pace, not mechanically.
- Place whitespace between complete sections, not inside a related pair.
- Use full-width media only when scale or comparison requires it.
- Captions must state the takeaway, not merely name the artifact.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prioritize:

1. outcomes and observable change;
2. decisions and tradeoffs;
3. product/workflow artifacts;
4. research evidence;
5. process documentation;
6. decorative context.

Remove an artifact when it does not change the reviewer's understanding.

## Authorship language

Use verbs that accurately represent scope:

- **Owned:** “I defined,” “I designed,” “I prioritized.”
- **Led collaboratively:** “I led the team through,” “I facilitated.”
- **Influenced:** “I recommended,” “I shaped.”
- **Contributed:** “I partnered on,” “I supported.”
- **Team result:** “We shipped,” followed by the specific personal contribution.

Do not switch between “I” and “we” to make scope appear larger.

## Confidentiality

When work is sensitive:

- anonymize users and business figures;
- label redacted or recreated artifacts;
- preserve the reasoning and relationship between evidence and decision;
- never fabricate realistic numbers to fill a gap.

## Length guidance

- Cover thesis: 20–35 words.
- Executive summary: 120–220 words total.
- Section intro: 40–100 words.
- Narrative paragraph: usually 60–140 words.
- Caption: 15–45 words.
- Full case study: as short as the evidence allows; remove repeated process narration.

## Anti-patterns

- Process theater: double diamonds, sticky-note walls, and method lists without decisions.
- Generic headings: “Research,” “Ideation,” “Solution” without a claim.
- Screenshot dumps and unlabeled UI galleries.
- Metrics in decorative cards with missing baselines.
- Treating tools as competencies.
- Hiding role and constraints until the end.
- A linear process story when the work was iterative.
- Ending with a celebratory launch and no reflection.

## Case-study acceptance criteria

- The abstract names the problem, contribution, and outcome or caveat.
- Scope and authorship are unambiguous.
- At least two consequential decisions are shown with evidence and tradeoffs.
- Every major visual has a caption and purpose.
- The scan path communicates the full story.
- Claims are supported and outcomes are qualified.
- Mobile reading order remains explanation before evidence.
- The page uses the shared template and tokens without a separate visual system.
